## The brief

A university needed a learning management system built from nothing - no legacy
platform to extend, no existing data model to inherit. That is either a gift or a
trap depending on whether you spend the first weeks deciding what the system is
*not*, and we spent them that way.

## What I owned

As architect I owned the front-end architecture end to end:

- the **data model and API contract** the UI would be built against
- **routing and state** strategy for a heavily nested course → module → lesson
  hierarchy
- the **component library** that kept twelve engineers writing consistent UI
- review, standards and the accessibility baseline

<Callout type="info" title="The part that actually mattered">
With a team of twelve, consistency beats cleverness. A boring, well-documented
component library did more for delivery speed than any individual optimisation.
</Callout>

## Leading twelve engineers

Directly managing a front-end team of that size is mostly an architecture
problem in disguise. The decisions that mattered were about **interface
boundaries** - who owns which surface, what the contract is between them, and
how a change in one area can avoid forcing a change in another.

That meant:

- owning the review culture rather than reviewing everything myself
- writing down decisions, because twelve people cannot hold the same context
- refusing scope that would have broken the model we had committed to

## Outcome

A scalable, high-performance UI platform that kept shipping after the initial
delivery - which is the only real test of an architecture.
